\chapter*{Abstract}

This thesis describes the creation of the video game Moons\&Stars, a space exploration and shooter game that features procedurally generated planet, giving it a unique game world. The players can interact within a completely procedurally generated universe, that will give the game an infinite number of possible worlds to play in, while also eliminating the need for manual content creation.

There were many challenges that had to be tackled during the creation of the game. This paper goes through the solutions given to these challenges and also discusses why each of them was chosen in order to tackle that given problem instead of other alternatives. It will discuss topics related to procedural generation, sphere creation, implementing a level of detail system, texture mapping and networking for multiplayer.

The main focus and biggest challenge of the work is the planet generation system. While exploring multiple options for the generation of spheres, the cube-sphere method is selected for its simplicity and for its ability to be easily subdivided or merged back for the application of level-of-detail techniques. The level of detail system makes use of quadtrees in order to efficiently facilitate the changes between resolutions in real time on the parts of the visible sphere being closest to the viewing camera. In order to form the simple spheres into unique and realistic looking planets with realistic looking terrain, Perlin-noise and other mathematical algorithms are applied over the spheres. Different simple mathematical functions are explored in order to create the shapes needed to form the terrain from the spheres.

\section{Motivation}

This thesis is motivated to explore the foundations and use of such algorithms on spherical objects, with a very heavily varying level of detail, providing both an explorable universe of planets and playable individual worlds at the same time. It wants to find ways to generate assets and game worlds for use without costly manual modelling of 3D assets. The use of procedural generation in space games have been explored before but their use in multiplayer, small indie creations remains limited. 

This work implements procedural planet generation inside a small indie creation, inside a strictly multiplayer creation and to analyze the techniques viability in small scale multiplayer creations. The goal was developing an enjoyable multiplayer game with minimal texturing and modelling work done. This reduction in assets can make it possible for very small scale teams, or even single creators, possible to create commercial quaility games.

\section{Challenges}

The first challenge to be tackled in this work is the generation of a single sphere. Multiple sphere generation methods are explored, each with their drawbacks and advantages \cite{doliveira2018} . \section{Previous work}

The application of procedural content generation within commercial game architectures has evolved from an early optimization strategy to a potential fundamental design paradigm. As outlined in the comprehensive survey made by Hendrikx et al. \cite{hendrikx2013}, PCG can be employed across multiple layers of virtual environments: ranging from low-level geometric data to high-level systemic mechanics. The primary driver for modern PCG integration is the mitigation of skyrocketing production costs associated with high-fidelity asset modeling \cite{smelik2014}. Traditional development structures increasingly struggle under the financial burden of manual asset creation, allowing algorithmic synthesis to bridge the resource gap for independent developers who require expansive virtual ecosystems \cite{short2017, korn2017game}. Early foundational mechanics demonstrated by Smith et al. \cite{pcgsmith2012} confirm that when PCG is integrated directly into the core gameplay loop, it can completely redefines spatial exploration and player strategy.

The core challenge of rendering large scale procedural words is the efficient management of compute and memory resources while also providing the player with pleasing visuals. This requires dynamic level of detail (LOD) systems. The conceptual foundation of hierarchies with multiple resolutions was introduced by Clark \cite{clark1976}, establishing that computation times should scale linearly with visible scene complexity rather than total world geometry. For heightfield rendering and expansive terrain structures, continuous LOD algorithms became standard, allowing seamless mesh transitions to prevent visual popping artifacts \cite{lindstrom1996}. Modern implementations have migrated these approaches onto modern hardware architectures, exploiting GPU-based continuous chunk allocations to optimize real time performance thresholds \cite{hugues2005, Wang2020LevelOD}. In contemporary engine pipelines, these systems rely heavily on quadtree data structures to dynamically subdivide terrain nodes based on their distance to the player camera \cite{wu2010, deaconu2021}.

Shifting to a sphere geometry from flat terrain often causes distortion if algorithms aren't carefully selected or adapted. Consequently, specific research has focused on alternative grid arrangements, such as the development of novel coordinate systems for constructing balanced spherical grid topologies \cite{lei2020}. To establish cohesive multi-resolution terrain directly on these spherical models, d'Oliveira and Apolinario Jr. \cite{doliveira2018} successfully adapted planetary-scale multi-resolution frameworks tailored for real-time video games. This structural combination allows developers to reconcile macroscopic celestial navigation with localized, high-fidelity ground interaction. A recent comparative analysis by Zechmann and Hlavacs \cite{zechmann2025} systematically evaluates modern procedural planet generators, highlighting the performance tradeoffs between memory overhead and real-time generation speeds across different client architectures.

To inject naturalistic complexity into these spherical meshes, stochastic noise algorithms are universally applied to simulate geological patterns. The historical baseline for this technique remains Perlin noise, introduced by Ken Perlin \cite{perlin1985} to synthesize pseudo-random textures through coherent gradients. This was later optimized into Simplex noise to correct second-order interpolation discontinuities and gradient distribution issues \cite{perlin2002}. Within contemporary terrain pipelines, these noise variants are layered fractally via octaves, altering the overall landscape roughness and lacunarity \cite{rose2016}. As documented in a recent literature review on the evolutionary trajectory of Perlin noise and its mathematical improvements \cite{wu2024}, modern pipelines routinely combine these gradient noise functions with secondary mathematical filters to accurately mimic authentic tectonic and erosive formations.

\section{Procedural generation in games}

Procedural generation refers to any technique where creation of content happens through algorithms rather than manual work of humans \cite{short2017,rose2016,pcgsmith2012,hendrikx2013}. The technique is long used in video games and is also used in many of the most popular games of the market. It has many different uses that can enhance games in very different ways. It is also often used to provide infinite replayability in open ended games. 

\subsection{Types of procedural content}

Procedural content can be roughly categorized into several distinct type, each having a different purpose inside games \cite{pcgsmith2012, korn2017game}. First, most common use is the creation of game terrains, realistic or fantasy landscapes, any kind of playable world as place of player interactions. Use in level generation is also common, producing game levels, dungeons, or mission layouts, often filling them with procedurally generated loots and resources as well. Narrative generation is also often used for storytelling elements, giving uniqueness to the game's dialogues. Finally, texture and material generation is utilized in the production of terrain textures, surface details, unique and non-repeatable texturing of different objects.

This thesis focuses on terrain generation, more specifically terrain generation applied to spherical surfaces, and simple texture generation over those spherical words, inside a simple game world.

\subsection{Deterministic vs stochastic generation}

A deterministic system is one where no randomness is involved in the development of future states of the system. In procedural content generation, this often refers to purely functional generation, for example the use of mathematical noise functions like Perlin where the output is a direct mapping of some input coordinate and/or seed value. Because the output is fixed for any given input, these systems are inherently reproducible, allowing for consistent world states given the same set of parameters, circumventing the need to save vast amounts of data about the generated world for reproducibility.

In contrast, stochastic generation introduces the element of chance and randomness. However, in computing today "randomness" refers to pseudo-random number generators in general. These generators while at first might look completely unpredictable still usually are made up of mathematical algorithms dependent on an initial seed value. Their output is governed by this initial seed value.

So in conclusion, most "random" game worlds are using the seeded-stochastic approach that appears endless, random looking content with the added benefit of easy reproducibility through a single seed value. The game presented in this work employs this method as well for the creation of its worlds.

\subsection{Level of detail systems}

The most critical challenge in rendering very large scale game environments is in the management of computing resources. Nearby objects need a high enough resolution to be detailed in a visually pleasing manner, but that is very computationally costly and also unnecessary for objects being far away from the viewpoint of the player's camera. Level of detail (LOD) systems have long been used \cite{clark1976} to address this by reducing the resolution of objects based on their distance from the camera \cite{wu2010,deaconu2021}.
For rendering worlds on a planetary scale an effective LOD system is essential, even more so to leverage the potential of infinite terrain generation possibilities given by procedural generation. There are many traditional ways to solve this problem of transitioning between different resolutions.

On of them is discrete LOD, where we use pre-defined models at varying complexity levels that swap based on distance. Can be very effective for static assets and computationally very lightweight but needs many models, generally all created by hand and is poorly suitable to be used alongside procedural generation;

A good alternative is a continuous LOD system, consisting of dynamic mesh simplification that adjusts the number of triangles inside a mesh in real-time \cite{lindstrom1996}. More complicated to implement and somewhat more resource-intensive but highly adjustable. It is also very suitable to be used alongside procedurally generated content;

A very interesting solution is geometry clipmaps, a technique for rendering large terrains using nested grids centered around the viewer \cite{hugues2005}. This technique, while very CPU-efficient because it treats terrain as a set of scrolling buffers, it can be memory-intensive as it maintains details in a fixed radius regardless of the camera's orientation. Furthermore, it is a technique designed to be used on flat planes, applying it to spheres will result in a serious amount of distortion at the poles.

These approaches are widely used in terrain rendering and have been adapted for planetary scale environments \cite{doliveira2018, korn2017game}.

For spherical geometry, these methods must be adapted to account for the closed surface topology, a challenge explored in detail in the following section \cite{zechmann2025}.

\section{Sphere mesh generation techniques}

Creating a sphere from triangles is a non-trivial problem in computer graphics. There are many different methods, each with very distinct advantages and trade-offs regarding the distribution of vertices, memory usage and suitability for subdivision-based LOD systems. The work reviews multiple options and also argues why the solution implemented in this work was chosen.

\subsection{UV sphere}

The UV sphere, also known as a latitude-longitude sphere, is constructed by arranging vertices in horizontal rings (latitudes) and vertical segments (longitudes). This method is conceptually simple and produces a regular grid-like structure governed by the following equation \ref{eq:uv_sphere_definition}:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:uv_sphere_definition}
\begin{gathered}
    \mathbf{(x, y, z) = (r \sin \phi \cos \theta, \quad r \cos \phi, \quad r \sin \phi \sin \theta)} \\
    \text{where} \\
    \mathbf{\theta = u \cdot 2\pi} \quad \text{and} \quad \mathbf{\phi = v \cdot \pi}
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}

However, the UV sphere suffers from several significant drawbacks that heavily impacts its utility in procedural terrain generation. The first and biggest one is pole singularities: at the north and south poles, all meridians converge, creating triangles with increasingly poor aspect ratios \cite{lei2020}. This convergence leads to non-uniform vertex distribution: vertices are densely packed near the poles and sparsely distributed near the equator. This consequently also causes texture mapping distortion where the polar regions experience severe stretching when applying textures. These limitations make the UV spheres unsuitable for applications requiring uniform geometry, such as planetary terrain creation. 

\subsection{Icosahedral sphere}

The icosahedral sphere or icosphere, addresses the limitations of UV spheres by starting with a regular icosahedron - a polyhedron with 20 equilateral triangular faces — and than subdividing each triangle recursively. After the subdivisions, the vertices are normalized to lie on the unit sphere. This construction is governed by the recursive refinement and normalization process defined in Equation~\ref{eq:icosphere_definition}:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:icosphere_definition}
\begin{gathered}
\mathbf{V_{new} = \text{normalize}\left(\frac{V_a + V_b}{2}\right)} \
\text{where} \
\mathbf{\text{normalize}(\mathbf{v}) = \frac{\mathbf{v}}{|\mathbf{v}|} \cdot r}
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}

In this context, $V_a$ and $V_b$ represent the vertices of an existing edge and $r$ denotes the desired radius of the sphere.

This approach offers several advantages compared to the more simple UV sphere \cite{doliveira2018,zechmann2025}. First it features a niform vertex distribution. All of the triangles remain roughly equal in size and shape across the entire sphere.
Second, it eliminates singularities, so unlike on the UV sphere, there are no poles or degenerate regions where vetices concentrate. It also offers good texture mapping properties. The uniform geometry minimizes distortion when applying surface details. While the icosphere is more suited for applications of procedural generation inside games, it still does suffer from a highly complex indexing and subdivision logic and is poorly suitable for a level of detail system.

A cube sphere is generated by subdividing the faces of a cube and then projecting the vertices outward onto a sphere. This method produces a sphere with six distinct patches corresponding to the original cube faces. The initial cube structure used as the base geometry is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:cube_simple}. The cube sphere offers a compromise between the UV and icosahedral approaches. By constructing the sphere from 6 faces, it avoids polar distortions entirely because each face retains a highly regular grid structure. Has no pole distortions, each face has a regular grid structure. More so, texture mapping is ver easy with this approach: each face can simply be treated as a 2D texture region. Furthermore, LOD implementation is a lot simpler due to this structure as well, quad-based subdivision can be applied uniformly across faces\cite{doliveira2018}. However, the cube sphere introduces visible seams along the edges of the six faces unless special care is taken to ensure smooth normals and consistent texture mapping at the boundaries.

For this thesis, the cubesphere approach was selected due to its uniform vertex distribution and natural compatibility with recursive subdivision for dynamic LOD systems.

\section{Perlin noise and procedural terrain}

To transform a mathematically perfect sphere into a realistic planet, elevation variations and surface details must be applied. Perlin noise, developed by Ken Perlin in 1983 for the film \textit{Tron}, provides the foundation for most procedural terrain generation systems \cite{wu2024}.

\subsection{Mathematical foundations}

Perlin noise is a gradient noise function that produces pseudo-random, coherent values over continuous space \cite{perlin1985}. Unlike pure random noise, Perlin noise exhibits smooth transitions between values, creating natural-looking patterns. The algorithm operates by firs defining a grid of integer lattice points with random gradient vectors. Than for any input point, identifies the surrounding lattice points, computes the dot product between each lattice point's gradient vector and the offset from that lattice point to the input point. Finally, it interpolates these values using a smoothsetp function to produce the final result. Figure~\ref{fig:perlin_noise} show image of Perlin noise output on grayscale.

Perlin's later work \cite{perlin2002} introduced \textit{Improved Perlin Noise}, which addressed artifacts in the original implementation by using a better interpolation function and a more robust gradient selection scheme.

\subsection{Octave summation}

Simple Perlin noise produces patterns at a single scale, which often appear too uniform. To create more natural, fractal-like terrain, multiple layers of noise, usally called octaves are summed together. Each successive octave has double the frequency and half the amplitude:

\[
f(\mathbf{p}) = \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} \frac{\text{noise}(2^i \mathbf{p})}{2^i}
\]

This summation, known as fractal Brownian motion (fBm), produces self-similar patterns at different scales, mimicking the multi-scale nature of real-world geologic features such as mountains, hills, and rocks.

\subsection{Applications to planetary generation}

For generating planets, Perlin noise can be applied for multiple parts to dynamically shape the entire world \cite{hendrikx2013, doliveira2018, zechmann2025, korn2017game}. First, using 3D noise for terain elevation. Three-dimensional noise functions on the sphere's surface coordinates the continuous terrain elevation data. Another great use is biome distribution, different noise functions can determine climate zones, moisture levels, and biome types accross the planet. Then elevation, slop and biome information already generated with the help of Perlin noise can be combined in order to assign surface colors to the planet. Lastly, noise functions can also help determine the placement of any other objects that give additional detail to the planet, like rocks, trees and other similar features. By combining multiple noise functions with different frequencies and amplitudes, a wide variety of planet types can be created from simple barren rock, to lush worlds with intricate biomes and vegetation.

\section{Quadtree data structure}

A quadtree is a hierarchical spatial data structure \cite{wu2010, zechmann2025} used to partition two dimensional space by recursively subdividing it into four equally sized regions. Each node in the tree represents a rectangular region defined by the coordinates of its 2 corners on a diagonal (called minimum and maximum coordinates). If higher detail is required in a region, the node is subdivided into four child nodes. Consequently, the 4 divided nodes can be merged back to 1 single node in a similar manner.

Quadtrees are particularly suitable for terrain rendering \cite{wu2010,deaconu2021, zechmann2025}, as they allow adaptive resolution depending on the viewer's position. Regions closer to the camera can be subdivided further, while distant regions remain at lower resolution.

In this work, quadtrees are applied to each face of the cube sphere representation. Each quadtree can have an associated mesh, making up a patch of the created planet. It will either contain a mesh patch or have 4 children. This enables efficient management of level of detail across the entire planetary surface. In addition, each quadnode can be independently hidden and not rendered easily with all its associated children in case it is invisible to the camera, saving further computing resources.

\section{Level of detail for planetary rendering}

Level of detail (LOD) techniques are essential when rendering planetary scale objects. A player may observe a planet from orbit or stand directly on its surface, requiring vastly different levels of geometric detail.

\subsection{Distance based LOD}

In this work, LOD is determined based on the distance between the camera and the surface patch. Each node computes the distance from the camera from its center. If camera distances shrinks, patches are subdivided after a given threshold and until a maximum given resolution. When the camera gets further, they are merged beack after leaving the resolution threshold. 

This approach ensures that nearby terrain is highly detailed while at the same time it simplifies distant objects and ensures that performance remains stable regardless of viewing scale.

\subsection{Subdivision and Merging}

The LOD system is defined by the basic operations of subdivision and merging. Each time a higher detail threshold is hit below the maximum resolution, subdivision happens and each node is divided into 4 child nodes. In the case of division the 4 child nodes are removed and replaced with a lower resolution parent mesh when detail is no longer needed. This dynamic process allows real time adaptation of terrain resolution as the player moves through the environment.

This hierarchical subdivision process applied on cube faces is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:cube_lod}, where different levels of detail are visible depending on the region. When level of detail is applied to spherical geometry, the resolution dynamically adapts depending on the camera distance, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:sphere_lod}.

\section{User interface and input}

The user interface (UI) and user input handling for the main menu, matchmaking lobby, and in-game flight simulation are constructed using a decoupled, event-driven architecture designed around Unity's Canvas system and the modern Unity Input System framework. Rather than embedding core networking, physics, or aesthetic feedback logic directly into monolithic scripts, the architecture relies on a strict separation of concerns. UI presentation panels act as observers reacting to backend states, player input is abstracted through action maps to insulate physics from hardware configurations, and UI hover effects are implemented via a pluggable, data-driven ScriptableObject pattern.

Continuous actions, such as camera lookup vectors, sample input explicitly within the frame loop:
\begin{equation}
\vec{m}_{\Delta} = \mathbf{ReadValue}\langle\text{Vector2}\rangle()
\end{equation}
Mouse looking scales the coordinate delta against empirical sensitivity vectors and frame-rate boundaries ($\Delta t$) before accumulating raw values into Euler state tracking fields ($\theta_{\text{pitch}}$, $\phi_{\text{yaw}}$). The pitching orientation is strongly constrained using bounded functions:
\begin{equation}
\theta_{\text{pitch}} = \max(-30^\circ, \min(30^\circ, \theta_{\text{pitch}}))
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\psi_{\text{roll}} = \mathbf{LerpAngle}(\psi_{\text{roll}}, 0^\circ, \Delta t \cdot r)
\end{equation}

Physical translations are applied inside the physics loop (\texttt{FixedUpdate}) via Rigidbody velocity vectors. Linear velocity is computed along the ship's local coordinate axes and constrained to a normalized direction vector when combined magnitude threshold metrics are exceeded. \begin{equation}
\vec{v}_{\text{next}} = \mathbf{Lerp}(\vec{v}_{\text{current}}, \vec{v}_{\text{target}}, 5 \cdot \Delta t_{\text{fixed}})
\end{equation}

Combat weapon triggers are managed by the \texttt{OnFire} callback. When performed, the system validates the weapon's fire-rate cooldown constraints against the current engine timeline ($t \ge t_{\text{next}}$). If validated, it instantiates a predefined projectile prefab at a structural muzzle point, assigns it linear momentum scaled by initial muzzle velocities, adds the ship's existing structural vector velocity to maintain kinetic consistency, and schedules an automated object destruction callback after a 5-second lifetime window.

\subsection{Data driven extensible UI feedback subsystem}

To provide rich visual feedback during menu navigation without cluttering UI state machines, a decoupled, component-based event framework handles object hover highlights. Built on Unity's \texttt{IPointerEnterHandler} and \texttt{IPointerExitHandler} interfaces, this system shifts visual configuration out of source code and relocates it into customizable Unity asset files.

The structural blueprint utilizes an execution engine split into three dependencies:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{\texttt{Hoverable.cs}}: A reusable component attached directly to UI GameObjects. It serves as an interaction interceptor that catches engine event data during pointer transitions and forwards the targeted object's references to a customizable list of abstract scriptable actions.
    \item \textbf{\texttt{HoverEffect.cs}}: An abstract ScriptableObject defining the operational structure for pointer entry and exit states. Because it inherits from \texttt{ScriptableObject}, configurations exist as standalone asset instances in the project files, allowing multiple UI views to reuse the exact same behavior configuration.
    \item \textbf{\texttt{HoverSprite.cs}}: A localized data container component attached alongside the hover script to save specific asset data, such as a UI element's original and targeted hover imagery.
\end{enumerate}

When a player hovers their cursor over a UI element, the \texttt{Hoverable} component catches the interaction and loops through its assigned list of modular effects:
\begin{verbatim}
public void OnPointerEnter(PointerEventData eventData)
{
    foreach(var effect in _hoverEffects)
    {
        effect.OnHoverEnter(gameObject);
    }
}
\end{verbatim}

An example of this extensibility is the \texttt{SpriteChangeHoverEffect} class. When it receives an execution callback from a UI element, it performs a non-throwing component query (\texttt{TryGetComponent}) to safely look for both the visual renderer component (\texttt{Image}) and the data container script (\texttt{HoverSprite}). If both are found, it swaps the current asset texture with the hover-state variant. When the cursor exits, the pipeline reverses the change and cleanly restores the original sprite asset. This architecture makes it simple to add new hover behaviors, such as audio cues or scale transformations, by writing a new subclass without needing to modify existing menu logic.

\section{Planet generation system}

This section discusses in detail how one of the core features of the game, procedural planet generation, is implemented. Architectural decisions, the reasoning behind them, system design, and explicit technical implementation solutions for the planet generation are all discussed in this chapter, together with some possible performance improvements.

\subsection{Functional requirements}
The system must provide four basic functionalities. Firstly, it must generate spherical objects at runtime. Secondly, these spherical objects must have a dynamically adaptable resolution based on camera distance. Thirdly, a terrain with varying height levels should be applied over these spheres, generated with the help of Perlin noise. 
The fourth and final requirement is that a texture scheme, with colors predefined by terrain height, must be applied over these planets for a visually striking appearance. 

\subsection{System architecture}

The planet generation system is subdivided into two major components: the Mesh Generation Engine, and the Material and Color Generation Engine. These two main subsystems interact to compose the comprehensive planetary body. Sphere creation, LOD quadtree subdivision/merging, and structural terrain offsets are handled by the mesh generation pipeline, while color assignment based on specific terrain features is managed by the color generation layout.

\subsection{Mesh generation}

The underlying geometric framework of each procedural planet is built upon a normalized cube-sphere projection template. Standard UV spheres introduce geometric singularities and severe vertex crowding at polar coordinates, causing noticeable texture stretching and wasteful vertex processing. The cube-sphere strategy maps six separate, flat coordinate planes around an un-deformed bounding cube matrix, where each face is oriented along an explicit principal direction vector:
\begin{equation}
\vec{D} \in \{\vec{U}, \vec{D}, \vec{R}, \vec{L}, \vec{F}, \vec{B}\}
\end{equation}

Instead of pre-allocating large, static, high-resolution mesh buffers that would rapidly deplete system memory resources, each individual cube face scales its geometric density independently through an active quadtree data structure implemented via the \texttt{QuadNode} container class. The roots of these six face trees are owned authoritatively by the parent \texttt{PlanetMeshGenerator} entity.

The Level of Detail (LOD) transitions are monitored, calculated, and executed by a dedicated asynchronous tracking script named the \texttt{LodCalculator}. To guarantee that continuous distance calculations do not introduce thread blocks or frame stalls on the main execution thread, the calculator splits its tracking operations into two independent, time-gated coroutine loops:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{\texttt{CheckVisiblePlanets}}: Executes continuously at a specified interval ($t_{\text{update}} = 0.2\text{s}$) to perform a coarse physical spatial search (\texttt{Physics.OverlapSphere}) within a defined operational sweep radius ($R = 1000\text{m}$) anchored to the player ship's transform position. Objects bearing the tracking tag \texttt{"Planet"} force a parent component lookup to populate a dynamic consideration buffer (\texttt{m\_consideredGenerators}).
    \item \textbf{\texttt{CheckLodOnPlanets}}: Iterates backward over the active planet considerations, computing the precise Euclidean distance from the player to the target planet's core. It matches this distance against a sorted array of configuration steps (\texttt{m\_recalculateDistances}) to select the appropriate distance step boundary ($d_{\text{step}}$):
    \begin{equation}
    d_{\text{step}} = \min \{ d \in m_{\text{recalculateDistances}} \mid \|\vec{P}_{\text{player}} - \vec{P}_{\text{planet}}\| < d \}
    \end{equation}
    This targeted distance step value is written directly to the planet's data properties via \texttt{SetRecalculateDistance}, directly preceding an immediate execution call to \texttt{UpdateLod()}.
\end{enumerate}

When a node evaluation determines that a node's distance layer is smaller than its depth-allowed threshold and its structural level is smaller than the upper resolution bound (\texttt{m\_maxLevel}), the node triggers \texttt{SubdivideMesh()}. The subdivision routing instantiates four fresh child \texttt{QuadNode} configurations, incrementing the local depth parameter level ($l+1$):

\begin{verbatim}
Vector2 center = (m_minCoords + m_maxCoords) * 0.5f;
divisions[0] = new QuadNode(m_minCoords, center, m_level + 1, parent, mat);
divisions[1] = new QuadNode(new Vector2(center.x, m_minCoords.y), 
                            new Vector2(m_maxCoords.x, center.y), m_level + 1, ...);
divisions[2] = new QuadNode(new Vector2(m_minCoords.x, center.y), 
                            new Vector2(center.x, m_maxCoords.y), m_level + 1, ...);
divisions[3] = new QuadNode(center, m_maxCoords, m_level + 1, parent, mat);
\end{verbatim}

Each newly spawned child node immediately schedules mesh vertex generation loops to compute its localized high-resolution terrain sub-grid. To maintain physics and collision tracking accuracy as the player approaches the planetary surface, the resulting generated mesh data reference is passed simultaneously into the child node's visual \texttt{MeshFilter} component and its non-convex physics \texttt{MeshCollider.sharedMesh} field. Once the child elements are fully generated and active, the parent node clears its own mesh filter and disables its active visibility state to avoid duplicate rendering overhead.

Conversely, when the player moves away from the surface, the distance bracket increases, and the generator initializes a node cleanup pipeline via \texttt{MergeMesh()}. The parent node invokes a recursive traversal function named \texttt{DestroyChildren()}, which executes immediate \texttt{GameObject.Destroy} calls on the child mesh objects, frees the references from memory, restores the local low-resolution parent mesh filter, and toggles its active visibility state back on.

\subsection{Terrain generation and noise synthesis}

Once the quadtree settlement cycles finish processing the active geometric layouts, the vertex positions are deformed to transform the perfect sphere into an intricate planetary landscape with mountains, plains, and ocean floors. Vertex positions are initially mapped along a flat grid based on an index layout loop bounded by the mesh resolution parameter (\texttt{m\_resolution}). These flat positions are then mapped onto a perfect unit sphere surface using mathematical normalization:
\begin{equation}
\vec{V}_{\text{unit}} = \frac{\vec{V}_{\text{raw}}}{\|\vec{V}_{\text{raw}}\|}
\end{equation}

Topological height fields are computed by feeding these normalized coordinates into a data-driven pipeline managed by the singleton class \texttt{PlanetTerrainGenerator}. The generator evaluates an array of customizable scriptable objects configured using the \texttt{NoiseConfiguration} container, which pairs a specific \texttt{NoiseFilter} module with a detailed parameter profile (\texttt{NoiseDetails}). To support varied landscapes, the system evaluates this collection sequentially using a conditional masking system:
\begin{equation}
H(\vec{V}_{\text{unit}}) = \sum_{i=0}^{N-1} E_i(\vec{V}_{\text{unit}}) \cdot M_i
\end{equation}

Where the structural masking factor ($M_i$) scales dynamically based on the configuration profiles assigned to the target tracking array:
\begin{equation}
M_i = \begin{cases} 
1, & i = 0 \\
E_0(\vec{V}_{\text{unit}}), & i > 0 \land \text{useFirstLayerAsMask} = \text{true} \\
1, & i > 0 \land \text{useFirstLayerAsMask} = \text{false}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

This masking mechanism allows the first noise layer to establish macro-level geographic features—such as deep ocean basins or massive continental shelves—and restricts subsequent high-frequency noise filters from generating mountain ranges in the middle of deep ocean beds. Individual layers evaluate terrain details using specialized implementations of the abstract base class \texttt{NoiseFilter}, which encapsulates an internal instance of a three-dimensional Simplex noise evaluation engine.

\subsubsection{Simple noise filter}
The \texttt{SimpleNoiseFilter} samples coordinates by accumulating fractions of fractional Brownian motion (fBm) over a set number of octave layers. This creates organic, varied terrain shapes like rolling hills and plains:
\begin{equation}
E_{\text{simple}}(\vec{V}_{\text{unit}}) = \left[ \max\left(0, \sum_{j=0}^{L-1} \frac{\mathcal{N}(\vec{V}_{\text{unit}} \cdot f_j + \vec{C}) + 1}{2} \cdot a_j - v_{\text{min}}\right) \right] \cdot S
\end{equation}
Where $\mathcal{N}$ represents the underlying mathematical evaluation derived from the \texttt{Noise} library, $\vec{C}$ defines center offsets, $v_{\text{min}}$ limits the lowest values to establish ocean floors, and $S$ sets the total filter strength. Frequency ($f_j$) and amplitude ($a_j$) step updates scale across each layer step via roughness ($r$) and persistence ($p$) factors:
\begin{equation}
f_j = f_0 \cdot r^j, \quad a_j = a_0 \cdot p^j
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Ridged Noise Filter}
To create sharp, jagged geological structures like mountain ranges, the system uses the \texttt{RidgedNoiseFilter}. It modifies the standard noise sequence by taking the absolute value of the raw noise signals and inverting them relative to a set ridge offset value:
\begin{equation}
E_{\text{ridged}}(\vec{V}_{\text{unit}}) = \frac{\sum_{j=0}^{L-1} \left( o_{\text{ridge}} - |\mathcal{N}(\vec{V}_{\text{unit}} \cdot f_j)| \right)^{k} \cdot a_j}{\sum_{j=0}^{L-1} a_j} \cdot S
\end{equation}
Here, $o_{\text{ridge}}$ controls the sharpness boundary offset threshold, and $k$ determines peak sharpness. The resulting structural height value ($H$) scales the unit vertex position outward, generating the final local coordinate positions for the planet's terrain:
\begin{equation}
\vec{V}_{\text{final}} = \vec{V}_{\text{unit}} \cdot \left( R_{\text{planet}} + H(\vec{V}_{\text{unit}}) \right)
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Erosion simulation filter}
To transition procedural landscapes beyond artificial mathematical forms into realistic, weathered formations, the \texttt{ErosionSimulationNoiseFilter} executes an integrated hydraulic-style approximation script. It constructs a multi-octave initial landmass shape and dynamically computes structural slopes ($\nabla h$) across adjacent points via a localized central differences framework:
\begin{equation}
\nabla h = \left( \frac{\partial h}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial h}{\partial y}, \frac{\partial h}{\partial z} \right)
\end{equation}
The slope parameter is evaluated around an architectural step index offset ($\epsilon = 0.01$):
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial h}{\partial x} \approx \frac{E_{\text{raw}}(\vec{V} + \epsilon\hat{i}) - E_{\text{raw}}(\vec{V} - \epsilon\hat{i})}{2\epsilon}
\end{equation}
The absolute slope magnitude $\|\nabla h\|$ dictates localized sediment dissolution metrics. Higher gradients decrease the retention capacity ($K_{\text{erosion}}$), while flat plains capture the resting residue via a configurable sedimentation rate scaling script ($K_{\text{sediment}}$):
\begin{equation}
E_{\text{erosion}}(\vec{V}) = E_{\text{raw}}(\vec{V}) \cdot (1 - \|\nabla h\| \cdot K_{\text{erosion}}) + \|\nabla h\| \cdot K_{\text{sediment}} \cdot 0.5
\end{equation}
This allows the engine to naturally generate carved canyons and alluvial deposition basins along cliff valleys.

\subsubsection{Voronoi noise filter}
To introduce distinct geological cellular patterns---such as fractured tectonic plates, cracked desert mud floors, or sharp basalt columns---the system incorporates the \texttt{VoronoiNoiseFilter}. This module slices three-dimensional coordinate spaces into discrete cellular zones. Given a point vector ($\vec{V}$), the algorithm queries nearby integer grid boundaries to sample feature position configurations distributed across a cell matrix:
\begin{equation}
F_1 = \min_{i} \|\vec{V} - \vec{P}_i\|, \quad F_2 = \min_{i \neq \text{index}(F_1)} \|\vec{V} - \vec{P}_i\|
\end{equation}
The module exposes an execution switch statement map (\texttt{VoronoiReturnType}) to scale the geometric output configuration:
\begin{equation}
E_{\text{voronoi}}(\vec{V}) = \begin{cases} 
F_1, & \text{Type} = \text{F1 (Standard Cell Beds)} \\
F_2, & \text{Type} = \text{F2 (Smoothed Outcrops)} \\
F_2 - F_1, & \text{Type} = \text{F2-F1 (Sharp Ridges/Tectonic Faults)} \\
F_1 \cdot F_2, & \text{Type} = \text{F1}\times\text{F2 (Polygonal Clusters)}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
By computing exponents over these distance bounds ($\text{result}^k$), the system generates steep valley basins or flat mesa landforms with crisp, distinct boundaries.

\subsubsection{Frustum culling optimization engine}
To maintain stable frame rates when millions of procedural vertex positions are calculated and evaluated across deep level-of-detail quadtrees, the mesh generation pipeline incorporates a non-blocking geometry culling pass governed by the \texttt{FrustumCuller} component. Computing vertex transformations, height field lookups, and colliders for thousands of patches situated behind the player's view envelope creates severe processing overhead. 

The tracking loop implements an asynchronous optimization gate that references the main viewing camera (\texttt{TargetCamera}). Every tracking window tick ($t_{\text{check}} = 0.05\text{s}$), or immediately upon sensing physical camera translation or orientation updates, the system invokes \texttt{UpdateFrustumPlanes()} to calculate the viewing space borders using six mathematical boundary planes:
\begin{equation}
\pi_k: \vec{n}_k \cdot \vec{x} + d_k = 0, \quad k \in \{0, 1, \ldots, 5\}
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{frustum_culling.png}
    \caption{Editor Scene view visualization of the frustum culling, showing camera view and how invisible parts aren't rendered.}
    \label{fig:frustum_culling_gizmo}
\end{figure}

Before allowing a child \texttt{QuadNode} patch to trigger expensive mesh generation routines, the parent \texttt{PlanetMeshGenerator} queries \texttt{IsBoxVisible} by supplying the specific node's local bounding metrics (\texttt{center}, \texttt{extents}). The system validates visibility by calculating the dot product of the node's geometry bounds against the normal vectors ($\vec{n}_k$) of the viewing frustum planes:
\begin{equation}
r = e_x \cdot |n_{k,x}| + e_y \cdot |n_{k,y}| + e_z \cdot |n_{k,z}|
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\text{Visibility} = \forall k \in \{0, \dots, 5\}: \left( \vec{n}_k \cdot \vec{x}_{\text{center}} + r \ge -d_k \right)
\end{equation}

If this verification check fails for a plane, the cell is flagged as completely outside the visual envelope. The engine intercepts its generation pipeline and culls the cell immediately, eliminating redundant Simplex noise sampling and vertex configuration operations before they ever touch the execution pipeline.

To facilitate developer iterations on these noise profiles and visibility boundaries, the system overrides default inspector interfaces via a highly specialized editor suite (\texttt{PlanetEditor.cs}). The asset script binds a structural hook into Unity's editor lifecycle (\texttt{OnInspectorGUI}), drawing responsive parameter groups and exposing configuration properties. 

If any changes are detected within the inspector controls (\texttt{GUI.changed}), the inspector initiates automated background restructuring cycles via \texttt{RegeneratePlanet()}. To capture and export successful procedural landscapes, the editor provides an automated static backup bridge using structural baking commands, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:planet_editor_inspector}:
\begin{verbatim}
CombineInstance[] combine = new CombineInstance[allMeshes.Count];
for (int i = 0; i < allMeshes.Count; i++) {
    combine[i].mesh = allMeshes[i];
    combine[i].transform = Matrix4x4.identity;
}
combinedMesh.CombineMeshes(combine);
AssetDatabase.CreateAsset(combinedMesh, path);
\end{verbatim}
This creates an optimized asset file directly inside the workspace folders, allowing development builds to bypass expensive runtime evaluation routines during standard testing.

\subsection{Material and color generation}

Once the structural shape of a mesh face is established, the \texttt{PlanetColorizer} component handles surface colorization. To support rapid prototyping and automated variety, the system can either bind to preset surface configurations via the \texttt{PlanetType} enum or use an integrated color generation system.

When a randomized configuration is requested, the system uses an isolated initialization step to build high-contrast color arrays dynamically:
\begin{verbatim}
float HueMin = Random.Range(0f, 1f);
float HueMax = Mathf.Clamp01(HueMin + m_colorVariation);
Color SurfaceColor = Random.ColorHSV(HueMin, HueMax, 0.4f, 0.8f);
\end{verbatim}
The color generator populates a list of sorted data elements (\texttt{HeightColorPair}). To prevent layout sorting issues when querying colors later, the collection is sorted sequentially by height using lambda comparison expressions:
\begin{verbatim}
m_heightColorPairs.Sort((x, y) => x.height.CompareTo(y.height));
\end{verbatim}

Shading profiles are mapped onto the generated mesh by processing vertex data loops sequentially. The colorizer reads the evaluated topological displacement values from each vertex position. The color selection logic loops through the sorted height-color pairs, evaluating the appropriate color using linear interpolation (\texttt{Color.Lerp}) inside the active height band:
\begin{equation}
T_{\text{lerp}} = \frac{H_{\text{vertex}} - H_i}{H_{i+1} - H_i}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\text{Color}_{\text{vertex}} = (1 - T_{\text{lerp}}) \cdot \text{Color}_i + T_{\text{lerp}} \cdot \text{Color}_{i+1}
\end{equation}

If a vertex displacement value falls outside the defined array boundaries, the system uses fallback checks to clamp to either the lowest or highest color entry. The final color arrays are written directly into the mesh structure's vertex color buffer (\texttt{mesh.colors}), allowing custom shaders to color planetary surfaces efficiently in a single pass without needing separate texture sampling steps.

\subsubsection{Low overhead vertex shader pipeline}
To avoid the memory and GPU performance issues caused by sampling large, unique texture maps for hundreds of dynamic planet patches, surface shading is handled by a custom unlit vertex coloring shader program (\texttt{Custom/UnlitVertexColor}). This pipeline maps colors straight to the geometry by reading data directly from the low-overhead vertex color buffer array (\texttt{COLOR}).

The application feeds structural streams into the vertex input program (\texttt{appdata}), matching explicit local coordinate vectors with corresponding color values assigned by the \texttt{PlanetColorizer}:
\begin{verbatim}
struct appdata
{
    float4 vertex : POSITION;
    float4 color  : COLOR;
    float2 uv     : TEXCOORD0;
};
\end{verbatim}

The vertex processing module (\texttt{vert}) uses the built-in matrix multiplication functions to project local object coordinates straight into clip space positions (\texttt{SV\_POSITION}):
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{V}_{\text{clip}} = \mathbf{M}_{\text{mvp}} \cdot \mathbf{V}_{\text{local}}
\end{equation}
The pass-through mapping directly links the assigned vertex color property to the output structure data block (\texttt{v2f}):
\begin{verbatim}
v2f vert (appdata v)
{
    v2f o;
    o.vertex = UnityObjectToClipPos(v.vertex);
    o.uv = TRANSFORM_TEX(v.uv, _MainTex);
    o.color = v.color;
    return o;
}
\end{verbatim}

When rendering individual fragments (\texttt{frag}), the engine samples base background details from a texture reference and uses a multiplication pass to combine them with the interpolated color vector (\texttt{i.color}):
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{C}_{\text{final}} = \mathbf{T}_{\text{sampled}}(\text{uv}) \cdot \mathbf{C}_{\text{interpolated}}
\end{equation}
This allows the graphics hardware to render varied landscape gradients across dynamic level-of-detail changes in a single draw call, without requiring runtime texture updates or complex material variations.

\subsection{The Non-Linear Payoff of Visibility Filtering}
The updated performance metrics highlight a fundamental characteristic of visibility mechanics when working without compute shaders: visibility tasks involve a constant CPU tracking cost that scales linearly with node counts, but they yield a geometric savings in render overhead.

In the single-planet environment, the performance profile shows almost no variation between the baseline and culled runs ($2.33\text{ ms}$ vs. $2.31\text{ ms}$ in the \texttt{PlayerLoop}). At this scale, the processing time saved by omitting hidden vertices from the render pipeline is nearly equal to the time spent executing visibility checks on the CPU's main thread.

When the workload expands to five planets, however, the unculled \texttt{PlayerLoop} spikes up to $2.84\text{ ms}$. This performance loss is caused by pushing all generated geometry down the graphics pipeline, forcing the CPU to continuously dispatch draw calls for meshes that lie completely outside the camera's view field. 

Activating the \texttt{FrustumCuller} reduces this gameplay loop cost back down to $2.43\text{ ms}$ ($14.43\%$ reduction in loop duration). Every frame, the visibility manager extracts the six mathematical planes bounding the active camera's viewing frustum:
$$\pi_i = \begin{pmatrix} a_i & b_i & c_i & d_i \end{pmatrix}, \quad i \in \{1, \dots, 6\}$$
It then loops through the active \texttt{QuadNode} structures to verify spatial alignment by calculating whether the bounding box corners fall on the negative side of the planes:
$$\mathbf{n}_i \cdot \mathbf{c} + r < -d_i$$
Where $\mathbf{n}_i$ is the plane normal, $\mathbf{c}$ is the center point of the node's bounds, and $r$ is the calculated dot-product projection radius ($r = \sum |n_{i,k}| \cdot e_k$).

Even though these geometric calculations run sequentially on the CPU's main loop without thread parallelization, the minor tracking cost ($0.12\text{ ms}$ over the single-planet baseline) is far outweighed by the massive reduction in scene hierarchy overhead and engine rendering dispatches.

\subsection{Ecosystem Stability and Structural Lifecycles}
When looking across all configurations, the minimum processing time consistently stabilizes near $\approx 9.11\text{ ms} - 9.80\text{ ms}$. This bottom floor represents frames where the camera view is directed away from dense landscape geometry, dropping vertex transformations to a baseline level. 

Conversely, the maximum frame times expose structural performance spikes. When the player's camera triggers distance thresholds, a \texttt{QuadNode} instance splits into four lower-tier child nodes, or collapses up to a parent tier. Because these vertex arrays are generated, normal-mapped, and bound on the main CPU thread without asynchronous compute worker execution, the frame rate exhibits brief spikes (reaching up to $21.34\text{ ms}$ even when culled at scale). 

The fact that the filtered 5-planet maximum time ($21.34\text{ ms}$) remains significantly lower than the unfiltered 5-planet maximum ($28.40\text{ ms}$) proves that frustum filtering does more than just lower average render times; it actively minimizes the severe rendering spikes that can cause visible stuttering during fast camera rotations. This matches the target criteria established during the initial unit and integration testing validation phases.
